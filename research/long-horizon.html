Long-horizon jailbreaks and goal drift

What changes when an apparently benign conversation keeps going—and how should we evaluate the answers along the way?

Scope
Qualitative red-teaming · 15–30 turns
Reviewed
13 September 2026
In brief

In these exploratory exercises, early refusals sometimes gave way to qualified explanations and partial disclosure. That is a reason to inspect the whole conversation—not evidence that safety declines at a known rate as conversations grow.

The conversation is the unit of review

A request can change meaning as context accumulates. An educational opening, a discussion of edge cases, and a hypothetical follow-up may each look ordinary in isolation. A reviewer also needs to ask what the sequence is asking the model to do.

The question is not simply whether a response contains a refusal. It is whether the model maintains the relevant boundary while continuing to help with the legitimate task.

Follow the context, not just the last turn

The report describes four broad phases. This explainer compresses them into review points; it is not a transcript, a fixed turn schedule, or a simulated model.

What the report actually describes

The reported change is in response behavior: stronger refusal language early in some interactions, followed by conditional explanations and partial informational disclosure later. The source describes this as safety decay; the evidence here is qualitative and does not establish its frequency or cause.

“Goal drift” describes a mismatch between the initial framing and the direction of the exchange. It does not establish that the model’s internal objective changed. Conversational coherence and weak cross-turn intent tracking are hypotheses in the report, not isolated causal findings.

How the work was done

I conducted these individual, coursework-based red-teaming exercises during BlueDot Impact’s Technical AI Safety program, using non-production, publicly available models in controlled educational settings.

Conversation design
Roughly 15–30 turns, using educational context, boundary reasoning, hypothetical framing, and repeated clarification of intent.
Run conditions
The report specifies fresh conversation context, no system-prompt modification, and default decoding parameters.
Model coverage
Broad model classes are described, but exact identifiers and settings are omitted. This limits reproducibility and rules out a defensible model ranking.
Available artifact
A written report with sanitized prompt examples and qualitative summaries—not a complete, independently scored transcript dataset.

Keep three questions separate

A follow-up study needs an explicit rubric for useful assistance, harmful disclosure, and appropriate refusal. These review lenses are a proposed measurement structure, not metrics collected in the original work.

At each turn
What was asked? What was answered? Label assistance and boundary behavior separately; record when each first appears.
Across the path
Does earlier information become more consequential in combination? Preserve context and reviewer uncertainty. Counting disclosures alone cannot resolve composition.
At the endpoint
How does the conversation finish? A final refusal matters, but cannot retract information already supplied. Conversely, earlier refusal language does not settle the meaning of a later explanation.

Matched benign conversations are essential: a system that blocks every long exchange would prevent useful work too. The aim is appropriate boundaries, not refusal for its own sake.

What remains unknown

Useful contribution. A qualitative failure hypothesis, sanitized prompt progression, and a reason to evaluate responses in context.

Not established. A representative failure rate, a safety-versus-length curve, a causal explanation, or a ranking of models.

The report provides no automated metrics, validated turn-level labels, matched quantitative baseline, or uncertainty estimates. More turns also mean more tokens and opportunities to answer. Length cannot be treated as the cause without controlling for those differences.

Turn the observation into a test

  1. Match the task and resources. Compare the same objectives across direct and multi-turn formats, with audited token and call budgets and benign controls.
  2. Make the run reproducible. Freeze model versions, decoding, construction rules, and review horizons; retain complete transcripts.
  3. Validate the judgments. Use independent review and a human audit to label assistance, refusal, and ambiguous cases.
  4. Report the whole result. Compare endpoint outcomes with turn-level exposure, including nulls, failed runs, uncertainty, and benign-task costs.

The temporal-refusal-timing pilot takes this measurement question further. Its current outputs establish an engineering pipeline, not a validated behavioral effect.
