A red-team engine for agents

November 15, 2026

A harness for attacking tool-using AI agents, what it solved across two sanctioned engagements, where it failed, and what I am leaving out.

Why build one

Over two sanctioned engagements I attacked AI agents that read untrusted text while doing a job: one a multi-level capture-the-flag where an assistant handles chat, email, files and messages, the other an indirect prompt-injection work test on an email-triage agent. Both reward the same thing. The score is not what the model says. It is what the agent does: a reply that gets sent, a tool call that fires. That makes the work an engineering problem as much as a prompting one, so I built a harness around it.

How it is put together

The design rule is that generation and admission are separate. Attacker models only write candidate text. They never touch the browser, the session or any credential. A single submitter sends, and only after a rules-of-engagement linter has passed the candidate.

  • Generate. An ensemble of attacker models in several roles: generator, single-change mutator, disagreement probe and reviewer. A refusal detector watches each model, because a window that quietly turns into refusals looks exactly like a strong defence.
  • Gate. A linter blocks anything that is not one-shot and self-contained, that tries to obfuscate, or that smuggles in information the player should not have.
  • Rank and schedule. Spend the remaining budget on payload families nobody has sent yet, from families that have worked before. The deadline is an absolute instant stored on disk, so a crash cannot reset the clock.
  • Submit. A paced state machine with a host allowlist, so a session token cannot travel to another origin.
  • Grade and record. Deterministic grading that says "unknown" rather than guessing, a database that checkpoints every attempt, and a journal entry for every accepted break.

The code is about 6,600 lines with a test suite that pins the seams between stages. Around it sits a research layer: roughly 78 short-lived research agents over about 48 hours, none of which were allowed to fire a run.

Two of those numbers need context. Levels 2 and 3 passed on the first attempt only because I replayed payloads banked from the earlier engagement. That tests portability, not discovery. And in that first engagement the breaks came from payloads I wrote by hand, not from the generator ensemble. I count that as a finding about the engine rather than a flattering omission.

What actually worked

The useful parts were the loop and the forensics, not the generator.

  • Score the artifact. Every graded outcome came from a specific field the judge reads, such as a sent message body or a tool-call argument. Optimising the chat text was wasted effort.
  • Reframe, don't escalate. Wins made the target action the natural way to finish the task the user had already asked for. Stronger wording never beat that.
  • Belief is not obedience. The agents often believed a forged message and still declined to act on it. What changed the outcome was supplying the missing authorisation in a voice the agent already trusted.
  • Read why each run failed. On the work test, the jump from low single digits to 9 of 10 came from reading the refusal in every failed epoch and removing one objection at a time, not from new tricks.
  • Use pass math. With ten epochs per run, one variable per run and a pre-registered read, a 10 of 10 result is a lottery at a per-epoch rate near 0.9. I treated it that way.

Inferred: the agents resist instruction strength far better than they resist structure. The things that moved them were a changed task, not a louder command.

Where it failed

Level 6 stayed shut after roughly 1,900 attempts. The picture is of three compounding barriers rather than one missing trick, and my budget ran out first, so I cannot say it is impossible. The best remaining idea is untested.

On the work test, a residual refusal based on the agent's distrust of an unfamiliar domain never went away. I treat it as a floor for that design, not a bug to fix.

What I am withholding

I do not publish payload text, the specific weaknesses in how the targets built their context, grader details, platform internals, credentials, or the identities of the targets and models. A working payload is a working attack, and both platforms run assessments that are not mine to disclose. What is here is the shape of the engine and the aggregate results.

Method and limits

Figures come from my own run logs and the platforms' attempt histories. Claims marked inferred are my interpretation.

  • Two engagements by one person are an anecdote, not a benchmark.
  • Attempt counts mix hand-written, ensemble and replayed payloads, so they do not measure the generator alone.
  • Some captures are duplicated or superseded across runs, so attempt counts are approximate.
  • Agent behaviour is stochastic; one passing run does not establish a rate, and a failing run does not rule a design out.
