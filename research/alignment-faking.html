Training-aware compliance: a negative replication

Does telling a model that its answers will be used for training change what it refuses?

Evidence
N=200 pilot; N=400 paired replication
Reviewed
13 September 2026
In brief

A fixed-order pilot suggested a framing effect. In the larger randomized replication, both models differed by only 0.25 percentage points between conditions. That negative result is the main finding; changes in precision and generation mode mean order cannot be isolated as the sole explanation.

The claim being tested

Suppose a model is told that one interaction will be logged and used for training while another will remain private. If the model treats those contexts differently, its refusal behavior might change even when the harmful request is identical. The narrow observable is therefore a difference in refusal rates under deployment framing.

That observable is much weaker than a claim about motive. A rate difference does not reveal whether a model represents the framing, predicts training consequences, follows surface cues, or is responding to an unrelated implementation artifact. The study was designed to test behavioral sensitivity, not internal intent.

Experimental design

The study used DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct with harmful-behavior prompts from HarmBench. Generation was deterministic: temperature 0, greedy decoding, and a maximum of 128 output tokens. A lexical rule set labeled refusals.

McNemar’s test is appropriate for the asymmetry of paired binary changes. It asks whether “refuse only in logged” and “refuse only in private” occur equally often. Aggregate refusal percentages alone cannot answer that paired question.

Pilot and replication

The pilot produced a result that looked consistent with the initial hypothesis for DeepSeek: 18.0% refusal in the logged condition and 11.5% in the private condition. At the prompt level, 20 decisions changed from refuse to non-refuse in the tested order and 7 changed in the reverse direction; the reported McNemar p-value was 0.021. Qwen moved in the opposite direction, from 54.0% to 56.0%.

The N=400 replication changed the picture. DeepSeek refusal was approximately 1.0% versus 1.25%; Qwen was 5.75% versus 5.5%. Each model’s condition gap was only 0.25 percentage points, and reported paired tests were non-significant.

Primary conclusion

The stronger experiment did not support a practically meaningful deployment-framing effect in either tested model. The pilot signal should not be presented as a replicated alignment-faking result.

Why the signal changed

Randomizing condition order removes a simple systematic confound: every prompt no longer receives the same frame first. The large fall in prompt-level decision changes—from roughly 13–15% in the pilot to about 2.0–2.3% in the replication—also suggests that much of the original instability was not robust.

However, order was not the only change. The pilot used 4-bit batched generation; the replication used bfloat16 sequential generation. Quantization, batching, and implementation pathways can alter low-level output behavior. The study therefore supports “the pilot effect was protocol-sensitive,” but not the stronger causal sentence “fixed order alone caused the effect.”

What the study teaches

The negative replication is useful precisely because it changes the conclusion. Three practices emerge from it:

  1. Randomize paired condition order. Pairing is not enough when every pair is executed in the same sequence.
  2. Preserve inference settings. If a replication changes precision, batching, or generation code, treat causal attribution as unresolved.
  3. Inspect prompt-level flips. Aggregate rates can hide whether the same prompts are changing in balanced or asymmetric ways.

A better follow-up would use a small factorial design that crosses order, precision, and batching while keeping prompts, model checkpoints, decoding, and labels fixed. That would estimate which implementation choice contributes to instability.

Limitations and scope

What can be said

In the randomized N=400 experiment, the two framing conditions had nearly identical refusal rates for both tested models.

What cannot be said

The study does not establish that deployment framing never matters, nor that larger or closed models behave the same way.

Useful artifact

The paired protocol, prompt-level flip analysis, and negative result are reusable methodological evidence.

Missing evidence

No blinded human labels, stochastic decoding study, or isolated order/precision/batching ablation was completed.

  • Only two 7–8B open-source models were tested.
  • HarmBench emphasizes harmful requests rather than a broad benign and mixed-risk distribution.
  • Lexical refusal rules can miss nuanced compliance, partial assistance, and non-standard refusals.
  • Temperature-zero decoding does not characterize sampling variability.
  • Behavioral differences would not, by themselves, identify internal strategy or motive.
